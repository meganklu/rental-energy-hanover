Your rights

What New Hampshire requires of a landlord, what to do when an apartment is genuinely too cold, and who to call.

Legal: This site provides general information, not legal advice. Leases and situations differ. For a problem you are actually having, talk to one of the organizations at the bottom of this page.

The minimum heat standard

New Hampshire sets a floor. Your landlord has to be able to keep every habitableThe legal minimum condition a landlord must maintain for a home to be rentable at all.Read the full entry room at an average of at least 65°F. That is the law rather than a courtesy, and it does not depend on what your lease says.

"Habitable room" means a room you live in. A bedroom and a living room count. An unheated basement, a garage or a closet does not.

For utility service specifically, state rules define a winter period running from November 15 to March 31, which is when the strongest protections against losing service apply.

If your apartment is too cold

Work through this in order. Each step is also the record you will want if the next one is needed.

  1. Measure it. Put a thermometer at about waist height in the middle of the room, away from a window and away from the heat source. Write down the temperature, the date, the time, and the outdoor temperature. Do it more than once. One cold morning is a story, and a week of readings is evidence.
  2. Rule out the easy causes first. A blocked radiator, a thermostat set to a schedule you did not know about, or a heat source with furniture in front of it will all make a room cold without anything being broken. Start with keeping your heat source clear and finding your drafts.
  3. Tell your landlord in writing. Email or text, not a conversation in the hallway. Say what the temperature is, when you measured it, and what you would like done. Keep the thread. Writing it down is what turns "I mentioned it" into a date you can point at.
  4. Give a reasonable time, then follow up in writing. Repairs take a few days. Silence for a week is different from a repair taking a week.
  5. Get help. If the heat is still not there, the organizations below are who to call. Do not stop paying rent to force the issue, and do not assume you can deduct a repair from it. Both have specific legal rules, and getting them wrong can cost you your tenancy.

Utilities cannot be shut off to pressure you

A landlord willfully interrupting your heat, water or electricity is a prohibited practice in New Hampshire, and the statute attaches real remedies to it: actual damages, or $1,000 per day, whichever is greater.

This is about deliberate interruption. It is not the same as a heating system that has broken down, which is the repair problem above.

Safety comes before any of this

Safety: If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get outside to fresh air first, account for everyone, and call from outside. Never use a stove, an oven, a grill or a generator to heat a room. If you smell fuel or think a heating system is unsafe, leave and call the fire department rather than the landlord.

The Hanover Fire Marshal's Office handles fire prevention, inspections, and permits for gas and oil systems, and is the right contact for a question about whether a heating system in your unit is safe. 48 Lyme Road, 603-643-3342.

Programs a renter can actually use

Most energy programs in New Hampshire are written for whoever owns the building. A few reach renters, and one of them covers the full cost of weatherization if you qualify. Which ones apply to you, and what a landlord has to sign, are on the programs page.

Who to call

  • New Hampshire Legal Assistance gives free legal help to income-eligible tenants, and landlord-tenant work is one of its main areas.
  • NH Law About Winter Heating, from the New Hampshire Law Library, is a plain-language gateway to the statutes and utility rules this page summarizes.
  • Hanover Fire Marshal's Office for anything about whether a heating system is safe.
  • If you are a Dartmouth student, the Real Estate Office keeps the Upper Valley rentals list and links out to New Hampshire landlord-tenant law summaries. It does not inspect properties or vet landlords.
