Glossary

Every term this site uses, in a couple of sentences, plus why it matters to what you pay.

Baseboard electric heat
Also called: Electric baseboard, resistance heat
A heating element inside a long, low unit that runs along the bottom of a wall, warming the room directly instead of blowing air through ducts or circulating hot water.
Why you care: It is almost always billed straight to your own electric account, unlike heat that is included in rent, so how much you run it changes your bill directly. Electric heat, commonly baseboard, is the second most common heating type in Hanover rentals, after fuel oil. How to tell which kind you have.
Building envelope
Also called: Envelope, building shell
Everything that separates the inside of your home from the outside: exterior walls, roof, windows, doors, and foundation. Think of it as the shell around your heated space.
Why you care: Most weatherization advice, sealing and insulating and weatherstripping, is about closing gaps in the building envelope. Knowing the term helps the rest of the site make sense. You will rarely need to say it yourself, because this site points at the specific window, door, or wall instead.
Draft
Also called: Air leak
Air moving through a gap that should not be there. Cold air leaking in around a window or door, or warm air leaking out. You feel it as a cold spot or a faint breeze even with the windows closed.
Why you care: Finding your drafts is the first step before anything else on this site. You cannot seal a gap you have not found. Find your drafts.
Forced air, radiator, heat pump
Three ways heat gets delivered to a room, besides baseboard. Forced air pushes heated air through ducts and vents, and you feel it blowing. A radiator, steam or hot water, heats a metal unit that warms the room by radiating heat, with no fan. A heat pump moves heat rather than generating it, and can usually cool the room in summer too.
Why you care: Your heat type decides which improvements on this site apply to you. Sealing a duct only matters if you have forced air, and a radiator or baseboard should never be blocked or covered no matter which one you have. Understanding your heating system.
Habitability, minimum heat standard
Also called: Warranty of habitability
The legal minimum condition a landlord must maintain for a home to be rentable at all. In New Hampshire that includes heat: your landlord must be able to keep every habitable room at an average of at least 65°F.
Why you care: If your apartment cannot hit that standard there is a specific process for reporting it. What to do when a rental is too cold.
kWh
Also called: Kilowatt-hour
The unit your electric bill is priced in. It measures how much energy you used over time, not how much power something draws in a single moment.
Why you care: It is the number on your bill, and multiplying it by your rate, in cents per kWh, is how you see what something actually costs you. Read your bill.
Payback period
How long it takes for what you save on energy to add up to what you spent on the upgrade.
Why you care: On a 9-month lease, a $75 upgrade with a 3-year payback will not benefit you directly. This site favors low-cost, fast-payback improvements for exactly that reason.
Phantom load
Also called: Vampire draw, standby power
The electricity a device uses while it is plugged in but turned off or in standby. Chargers, game consoles, TVs, and anything with an always-on clock or light.
Why you care: It adds up quietly on a bill you are already paying, and it is one of the few things you can cut at zero cost and with no landlord permission. Cut phantom load.
R-value
Also called: Thermal resistance
A measure of how well a material resists heat flow. The higher the number, the better it insulates. It is printed on insulation, window film, and some weatherstripping packaging.
Why you care: When you are comparing two window insulation kits, a higher R-value means it will do more to keep the cold out.
Security deposit
Also called: Damage deposit
Money you pay your landlord up front, refundable at move-out minus the cost of damage beyond normal wear and tear.
Why you care: Any change you make that is not easily reversible risks part of that deposit. Every improvement on this site is flagged as reversible or not.
Split incentive
Also called: Split-incentive problem
What happens when the person who would pay for an upgrade, your landlord, is not the person who benefits from the lower bill it produces, you. Neither has a full reason to act on it alone.
Why you care: This is why a lot of good weatherization advice does not happen in rentals by default, and why this site separates what you can do yourself from what you have to ask your landlord for. Programs that pay for the work.
Thermostat setback
Also called: Night setback, temperature setback
Turning your heat down for a stretch of time, such as overnight or while you are out, and letting it come back up later, instead of holding one temperature all day.
Why you care: One of the easiest no-cost changes on this site, and it needs no landlord permission. A common misconception is that reheating a cold room costs more than leaving the heat on steady. The opposite is generally true: energy is wasted holding one temperature the whole time you are away or asleep, and that is the consumption a setback avoids. Set your thermostat back.
Utilities included
Heat, electricity, or both are already covered by your monthly rent, with no separate bill in your name for that utility.
Why you care: It changes almost every recommendation on this site. If your landlord pays the heating bill, sealing a draft mostly saves them money, not you. The improvements are still worth asking for, and they will not lower your own bill.
Weatherization
Also called: Weatherizing, weatherproofing, air sealing and insulating
Keeping indoor air from escaping and outdoor air from getting in, which is how you cut what heating costs. It means physical improvements to the building: adding insulation, and sealing cracks, especially in basements and attics. It is also what resolves ice dams, moisture, mold, cold spots and drafts.
Why you care: It is the umbrella term for most of what is on this site, and sealing and insulating are usually the cheapest ways to cut a heating bill.
