Understanding your heating system

Renter basics

The first question this site asks you is what kind of heat you have. Most people who have never paid a heating bill cannot answer it, and it is the answer that decides which advice applies to you at all.

You do not need to know how any of this is installed. You need to recognize what is already on your wall, and know what it costs to run.

Find it before you read the rest

Walk into the room you sleep in and look along the base of the walls, under the windows, and up near the ceiling. One of these is what you have. Most Hanover rentals burn fuel oil, and electricity is the next most common, so the first two are the likely answers.

  • Radiator

    A heavy metal unit, usually cast iron, under a window. No fan, no noise except an occasional knock or hiss.

    Water or steam heated by a boiler somewhere in the building, usually burning oil or gas. It is often on one system for the whole building, which means the thermostat is often not yours.

  • Baseboard

    A long, low unit running along the bottom of a wall, a few inches tall. Two kinds look nearly identical.

    If it has a dial or a switch on the unit itself, it is almost certainly electric, and it bills straight to you. If it is fed by pipes and controlled from a wall thermostat, it is hot water from the same kind of boiler a radiator uses.

  • Forced air

    Rectangular grilles in the floor, low on a wall, or in the ceiling. You hear a fan start and feel air blowing.

    A furnace heats air and a fan pushes it through ducts. Somewhere there is a larger grille that pulls air back to the furnace. This is the only kind where duct leaks matter, and they can be a large share of what you pay for.

  • Heat pump

    A wide white box mounted high on a wall, with a remote. Outside there is a fan unit connected to it by a thin line.

    A mini-split, which is one kind of heat pump. It is the only thing in this list that also cools the room in summer. If your apartment has air conditioning that is not a window unit, this is probably why.

The one that runs both ways

A furnace and a boiler make heat by burning fuel. A heat pump does not make heat at all. It moves heat that is already there, using refrigerant, the same way a refrigerator moves heat out of the food compartment.

In winter it pulls warmth out of the outside air, even cold air, and releases it indoors. In summer a reversing valve runs the whole thing backward, pulling heat and humidity out of the room and dumping it outside. That is the difference worth understanding, because it explains why a heat pump both heats and cools while nothing else on this page does.

What burning fuel looks like

A furnace or a boiler burns oil, propane or gas to make heat directly, then moves that heat into the home as warm air or hot water. Some of the energy always leaves as exhaust up the flue, and the system only runs in one direction. There is no way to reverse a furnace to cool a room in summer.

Do not close vents to save money

On a forced-air system, shutting the vents in a room you are not using seems like it should help. It does the opposite. Closing vents throws the system's balance off and makes the blower work harder against the resistance. If one room is always uncomfortable, that is a conversation with your landlord about the damper feeding it, not something to fix at the grille.

The same principle applies to every system on this page for a different reason: a heat source with furniture in front of it is heating the back of your couch.

True or false: your heat type does not really change what you should do about your bill.

False. Electric baseboard bills straight to you and is controlled room by room, so almost everything on this site applies. A radiator on a building-wide boiler may not even have a thermostat you can reach, and there your best moves are sealing drafts and asking your landlord.

Why it matters

Your heat type decides which half of this site is about you. It sets whether the bill arrives in your name, whether you can reach a thermostat, and whether the fuel price you should be watching is oil or electricity.

Tell the site which one you have, and it stops showing you the advice that cannot apply.

Who pays

Knowing what the system is only gets you halfway. The other half is whose name the bill is in, and that is in your lease rather than on the wall.

If heat is included in your rent, sealing a draft mostly saves your landlord money. It is still worth asking for, and it will not lower what you pay.
