One building, two sets of incentives

Almost every guide to a warmer, cheaper home is written for the person who owns it. In a rental, the person who pays to heat the place and the person who can change it are two different people. That gap is the reason this site is here.

You rent it

You pay to heat it.

What you are up against

  • The heating bill arrives with your name on it.
  • You cannot replace a window, insulate a wall, or change the heating system.
  • Your lease is measured in months. The equipment is measured in decades.
  • Asking for an upgrade means asking someone else to spend money that you would save.

What you can still do

  • Everything that comes off again at move-out, which is most of this site.
  • Choose how the heat gets used, which is free and often the largest single change.
  • Make a specific written request instead of a general complaint.
  • Point your landlord at a program that pays for their half.

They own it

They pay to improve it.

What they are up against

  • Insulation, windows and heating equipment are theirs to buy.
  • The savings turn up on a bill they do not pay.
  • A tenant who leaves in a year makes the payback period hard to justify.
  • When something breaks mid-winter, the cheapest working replacement is the fast one.

What changes the maths

  • Rebates and income-eligible programs that cover part or all of the cost.
  • A unit that is easier to rent, and cheaper to run between tenants.
  • Fewer cold-weather callouts and fewer complaints.
  • A tenant who asks in writing, with a specific ask and a program attached.

Economists call this a split incentive

The person who would pay for an efficiency improvement is not the person who would save from it, so the improvement often does not happen. Nobody has to behave badly for this to play out. It is what the arrangement rewards, and it is why advice written for homeowners stops being useful in its first paragraph when you rent.

So this site starts on your side of the line

  • Permission first

    Every improvement states whether you need to ask, and whether it comes off again when you leave. Those two answers decide whether advice is worth reading at all.

  • Who pays, and for what

    Tell the site who pays for heat and it stops showing you things that would save your landlord money and not you. A shorter shower saves nothing when hot water is included.

  • A specific ask

    Where a change needs the owner, the page says what to ask for, in writing, and which program to point at. Programs covers what is available in New Hampshire.

Hanover in particular

At the May 2017 Town Meeting, Hanover voted to move to 100% renewable electricity by 2030, and renewable heating, cooling and transportation by 2050. Sustainable Hanover records that Hanover was the first municipality in the country to make that commitment by popular vote.

Getting there means the rented half of the housing stock too. Rented homes in Hanover are heated mostly by fuel oil, then electricity, then piped gas and propane, and a large share of those tenants are students paying a heating bill for the first time.

What this site adds

Sustainable Hanover already publishes weatherization guidance for people who own a home in town. That guidance assumes a contractor, an attic and a basement, and a decision the reader is allowed to make. This site adapts the same ideas for people who are allowed to make almost none of those decisions, and adds the part a static page cannot do: you tell it your heat type and who pays for it, and it narrows what it shows you.

One of the committee's own programs runs the other way and is open to tenants directly. Window Inserts are held in by friction, with nothing screwed or glued to the frame, which is what makes them work in a rental.

How the content is sourced

Every number on this site traces to a named source, and each page lists its own at the bottom with the date it was last checked. Where no source exists, the number does not go on the page. Rates, rebates and program terms go stale faster than anything else here, so those pages carry the shortest review interval.

Content about tenant rights and about safety is reviewed by a person before it publishes. Neither is written from a search result.

The whole project is public, including the planning documents, the source list, and a record of how it was built. View the source code.

How AI was used

This site was built with generative AI. Anthropic's Claude Opus 5 wrote most of the HTML, CSS and JavaScript, drafted the explanatory copy, and drew the diagrams, working from written instructions and design documents and revising against feedback after each round.

Three things were held back from it. Facts are not taken from the model: every number, program term and legal statement comes from a named source recorded in the project's source list, and an automated check refuses to publish a page carrying a number with no source. Legal and safety content is reviewed by a person before it goes up. The photographs are not generated. They are existing photographs by named photographers, credited under each one.

Every instruction given to the model is recorded in the repository, in order, at docs/ai-use.md, along with the standing rules it works under in AGENTS.md.

What this site will not do

  • Name a specific landlord or a specific property.
  • Recommend a brand, or link to a product for commission.
  • Collect anything about you. Your situation and your reduce-motion setting are stored in your own browser and are never sent anywhere.
  • Advise you on a dispute you are actually having. That needs a person, and Your rights says who.

The disclaimers

Legal: This site provides general information, not legal advice. Leases and situations differ.

Savings: Estimates are typical ranges, not guarantees. What you actually save depends on your building, your heating system, and the weather.

Permission: Check your lease before making any change. When in doubt, ask your landlord in writing.
